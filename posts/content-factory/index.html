How to build a content factory

Coffeant, Nov 25 2026
Back

Most teams post when they have time, which is why the content never turns into users or revenue. A content factory fixes that: one input a week becomes a week of posts, clips and visuals, and every piece points at the same funnel.

Jack wrote the operating manual for it. These are my notes — condensed, with his numbers attributed, and a link to the original at the bottom.

1. The message comes before the machine

No system saves content built on a blurry message. Fill in one line first:

[project] helps [who] get [outcome]

His framing for that line: buyers pay for the margaritas in the Bahamas, the flight is just how they get there. Lead with the outcome, save the features for later.

Then pick 3–4 content pillars so people know what they follow you for, and every piece maps to one. The pillars are also the filter the trend scan runs through — the weekly angle is a trend that fits a pillar, not a trend that happened to be loud.

2. One input a week

The raw material is a single long piece: a founder podcast on video, a product demo, a customer call (with permission), or one long-form article on the thing you know best. Two minutes minimum, uploaded natively so people can clip it.

Then a trend agent runs weekly over what's moving in the niche, what competitors shipped, and the questions people keep asking. It returns a short brief on Monday, and the week's piece picks its angle from it. Add the material only you have: sales call transcripts, the changelog, customer wins with real numbers, the team's opinions.

3. The machine: a voice file and a ban list

Before any drafting, the model reads a few files:

  • A voice file — how the founder writes, built from their best posts (Jack's is built from 4,314 of his own).
  • A ban list — every word and structure you never want to see again. Every rejected phrase goes back in.
  • The offer and the audience — who you sell to and what they actually get.
  • Past campaigns — what worked, what didn't.

The flow is AI first draft → critic agent checks it against the ban list → a human writes the final, in their voice, with their real numbers. The last step isn't optional: he's explicit that AI should not write the final and should never write the replies.

4. The quality layer

A factory that produces a lot, fast, can produce a lot of slop fast. Four pieces keep the output recognisably yours:

  • A writing skill — the voice file, the ban list, and a real example of every format: short post, thread, article. A critic agent checks each draft before you see it.
  • A design skill — every piece of visual feedback you've ever given, written down: the "remove this", the "make it bigger". Plus brand colours, fonts, logo, one corner radius for everything.
  • A template gallery — one template per post type (big numbers, launches, partnerships, frameworks, stats, quote cards), brand applied, AI fills the copy.
  • A human check — every post and every visual read by a person before scheduling, every number checked against its source, every rejection fed back into the ban list or the design skill.

5. What comes out

One long piece becomes: 5–60 second vertical clips with captions and the hook as a text overlay; a thread with the best points; an article that goes deeper; visuals for the key numbers and frameworks; motion videos for launches and milestones (his team builds those in code from one template and swaps the copy).

The note on clips matters more than it looks: the first seconds decide whether people stay, so open on the point, not the intro.

6. Distribution is not the company page

  • The founder and the team post from their own accounts. A factory that only feeds the brand account is half wasted.
  • Everyone replies under the big accounts in their lane — 10–30 replies a day, in the first 15–60 minutes after a post goes up.
  • Clippers in the niche get the long video; creators get a one-page brief and write it their own way.
  • The email list gets the best piece of the week.
  • Everything is queued in one scheduler, each channel in its own format — vertical for TikTok, Reels and Shorts, text for X and LinkedIn.

On why replies are the cheapest growth lever, his numbers: replies score higher than likes in the ranking, back-and-forth with mutuals higher still. So write posts people answer and send to friends, not posts people nod at.

7. Turning it into revenue

Every piece points at one funnel: post → profile → landing page → convert.

  • Profile: the bio says what you do in one line; the pinned post is your best proof.
  • CTA: the link goes in the first reply, never the main post.
  • Landing page: one main button, real numbers, works on mobile.
  • Lead magnets: one per pillar — a guide, a template, a checklist, a free tool. Never "comment to get it".
  • Offer: one clear next step after the magnet — a call, a demo, a trial.

The cadence he runs: record Monday, AI drafts and a human edits Tuesday, thread and first clips Wednesday, article and more clips from team accounts Thursday, motion video and recap Friday. Replies every day.

What to track: signups, demos and meetings from content, revenue from those signups, profile visits and link clicks per post, which topics and formats bring leads. Impressions last — they're the easiest number to buy.

8. What not to do

  • Posting only when there's time, with no input and no plan.
  • Letting AI write the final post, or the replies.
  • The same post from every account.
  • Links in the main post.
  • Only the company page posting.
  • Tracking views and calling it growth.

9. What I'm taking from it

  • The ban list is the highest-leverage file in the system. It's the only artefact that encodes taste as a constraint rather than a comment, and it compounds every week.
  • "One input a week" is a capacity decision, not a content tip. It caps what the factory can produce per cycle and forces the choice of what the anchor piece is.
  • Impressions last — the single line that separates a content factory from a content habit.
