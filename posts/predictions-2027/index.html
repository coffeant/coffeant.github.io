Five marketing predictions for 2027

Coffeant, Nov 25 2026
Back

Stacy Muur has done GTM for a decade and wrote up her 2027 calls after reading 41 reports and studies. The short post covers five; the full newsletter has twelve. Most prediction posts are vibes — this one is unusually well sourced, so it's worth summarising properly.

My notes below, with her numbers attributed. Then where I'd push back, and what I'm actually changing because of it.

1. AI search brings PR and link building back

The mechanism is query fan-out: you ask one question, the model breaks it into sub-questions (“great restaurants, great bars, things to do if you have kids”, per Google's VP of Product for Search), searches each, then assembles one answer.

The number that matters: only ~12% of URLs cited by AI assistants rank in Google's top 10 for the original prompt (Ahrefs). Ranking first stopped predicting being quoted.

Her call: what wins is being mentioned across many places that answer those sub-questions, and having your brand sit as close to the topic as possible.

In practice that means the surface you need is not one page. It's the union of every page, profile and forum thread that answers a sub-question — which is exactly what a content factory with distribution is for, and why a single well-optimised landing page no longer does the job.

2. Banner blindness reaches creators

People learned to ignore banners; they're learning to ignore sponsored posts. In a Gartner survey of 1,006 US consumers, 35% say they rely on influencers less for product information specifically because AI-generated content is spreading (Gartner, Sept 2026).

Her read is not “influencers are dead” but “the bubble deflates into an efficiency zone”: one-off placements have weak ROAS, and the return comes from long-term collaboration (she points at IPA's analysis of 220 influencer campaigns on payback).

Her call: in 2027 brands cut one-off placements and move budget to fewer, longer creator partnerships.

The operational consequence is boring and hard: payback windows. A single placement you can judge in two weeks; a year-long partnership you have to model like a subscription, and most teams have no model for that at all.

3. Human-made content becomes a selling point

This is the sharpest section, because the numbers all point the same way:

  • 50% of US consumers prefer brands that avoid GenAI in consumer-facing content (Gartner, Mar 2026).
  • 57% say AI-generated content has made them less trusting of brand messaging (Gartner, Sept 2026).
  • 67% of UK consumers find AI-generated images of people in ads creepy (Optimizely).
  • In an Emplifi survey of what reads as authentic, AI-generated content ranked last, at 31%.

Her call: brands will use human-made content as a differentiator — original photography, real authors, sometimes an explicit “made by humans” label.

I'd add one thing she doesn't say: this turns a quality standard into a positioning claim. The moment “human-made” is a selling point, every AI draft you didn't rewrite becomes a liability rather than a shortcut.

4. Distribution is the scarce asset, not product

Building is nearly free now. GitHub logged close to 1 billion commits and 121 million new repositories in its 2025 reporting year, with commits up 25% year over year (Octoverse). Solo founders started 36.3% of new US startups in the first half of 2025, up from 23.7% in 2019 (Carta).

Her call: demand for customer development — and for access to power users through communities, panels and research networks — grows sharply in 2027.

A solo founder can change the product in a day, and cannot afford a wasted quarter. The cheapest growth path is therefore not more features: it's getting the right ten early users into a conversation and shipping their objections by Friday.

5. Personal brands drive company growth

Buyers want proof from people. In the same Optimizely UK survey, 72% of consumers say they're more likely to engage with content that feels human rather than automated. Her read: media presence shifts from the company account to the people who build the thing — founders first, then the team.

Her call: personal brands become a bigger growth driver in 2027, and companies increasingly pay for that value — a strong X, Instagram or Substack becomes part of a professional's compensation.

Where I'd push back

  • “PR is back” needs a measurement upgrade, not a budget line. If 88% of AI-cited URLs don't rank, then rank tracking cannot tell you whether PR worked. You need brand-mention tracking inside AI answers, or you're buying coverage and guessing.
  • “Human-made” is a claim you can only make once. One obviously AI-generated hero image and the label stops working. It's a constraint on the whole pipeline, not a campaign.
  • Creator partnerships get longer, which means fewer bets. Fewer, longer deals concentrate risk: pick wrong and you've bought a year of the wrong audience. Selection gets harder exactly as the deal gets bigger.

What I'm taking into 2027

  • The factory's input should be a customer call, not a trend scan. Her prediction 4 makes customer development the scarce resource; prediction 1 says sub-question coverage wins. One recording of a real user complaint answers both — it's evidence for the PR surface and the voice file at the same time.
  • Track citations, not rankings. Add one recurring job: ask the major assistants my category questions, record whether the brand shows up, and log which URL got cited. That's the new rank tracker.
  • Keep the human step exactly where the last post put it. AI drafts, human writes the final, human writes the replies. Prediction 3 turns that rule from a quality preference into the thing being sold.
  • Budget shifts: less one-off placement, more owned surface and long partnerships. Same money, fewer bets, longer clock — and a payback model instead of a two-week ROAS check.

Her own advice for the next year is the short version of all of it: be more selective with budget, test the new AI ad formats with explicit performance targets, and put the consistent money into PR, creator relationships, personal brands and distribution partnerships — starting early, because those relationships take the longest to build.
